# Ch 3 — How Do You Spend Yourself? (The Daily Planner)  ★ priority chapter

Citation convention: EPUB has no page numbers; cites are "Ch 3, §section"; page = n/a.

## 1. Main idea
ADHD is "a performance deficit" rather than a knowledge deficit, so intentions must be converted into scheduled actions. The Daily Planner (rows of time × columns of days) is an externalized "flash drive or the cloud for your brain" that holds all obligations and your To-Do tasks, and is meant to be used constantly (Ch 3, opening; §Choosing a Daily Planner).

## 2. Named methods / routines
**Choosing a Daily Planner and General Guidelines** — Take Away 3.1 (Ch 3, §Choosing a Daily Planner). Duration: not stated.
1. Decide paper vs. electronic; if in doubt, start with paper.
2. Pick a size/format fitting your scheduling demands.
3. Plan to "overuse" it: appointments, work/school, personal, recreational, self-care.
4. Err on the side of "underscheduling": leave buffer time between tasks and meetings.
5. Keep "task appointments" specific and behavioral.
6. Define start and end times whenever possible — "lower the bar."
7. Treat the planner as a "tool of daily life" with keys, wallet, phone.

Other named practices in the chapter:
- **(Over)use it or lose it** — use the planner for personal, social, recreational and discretionary time too, not only appointments (Ch 3, §(Over)use It or Lose It).
- **Self-care as scheduled tasks** — meals, sleep, exercise, reading, hobbies, downtime, breaks (Ch 3, §Self-Care Activities).
- **Underscheduling / "less-is-more"** — start small, add gradually (Ch 3, §Err on the Side of Underscheduling).
- **Start and end times for open-ended tasks** — e.g., 30 minutes on room cleaning instead of an 8-hour block (Ch 3, §Define Start and End Times). Rationale: realistic expectations, easier scheduling, structure for free days, guard against "brinksmanship."
- Cognitive rehearsal: building the Daily To-Do List and entering it into the planner rehearses and encodes the day (Ch 3, opening).

## 3. Tools / containers — one job each
- **Daily Planner** (paper recommended): the record of all obligations plus scheduled task appointments, referred to throughout the day (Ch 3, opening; §Choosing).
- **Paper planner**: one-view glance at day/week/month; encoding by writing; doubles as a "diary" of past schedules, e.g., last oil change (Ch 3, §Choosing).
- **Digital calendar**: portable, with alarms, but authors say it is under-checked and alarms get ignored "like car alarms"; works for a minority who are "wired in and synched up" (Ch 3, §Choosing).
- **Format range**: monthly one-square-per-day, up to day-by-day with 15-minute increments (Ch 3, §Choosing).
- **Daily To-Do List**: the source of tasks entered in the planner (Ch 3, opening).

## 7. Key terms (author's words)
- **Performance deficit** — difficulty converting intentions into actions; ADHD is "not a knowledge deficit" (Ch 3, opening).
- **Daily Planner** — "a tool for defining times in one's day, week, or month that are designated for action… as well as to arrange all of your obligations" (Ch 3).
- **Overuse** — use the planner for everything, not just formal appointments (Ch 3).
- **Underscheduling** — fewer tasks, more buffer than seems necessary (Ch 3).
- **Lower the bar** — set a reasonable time-boxed expectation instead of trying to raise motivation (Ch 3).
- **Choreography** — arranging tasks plus managing effort and energy through the day (Ch 3, §Self-Care).
- **Brinksmanship** — habit of working only at the last minute, with costs to health (Ch 3, §Define Start and End Times).
- **Planning is not doing** — a schedule is a blueprint, not performance (Ch 3, closing).
