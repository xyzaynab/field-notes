# Chapter 8. Self-Control: How to Get What You Want
Pages 64–68.

1. **Main idea.** Self-control is "the ability to choose something other than your initial impulse so you can get something in the future that you want more" (p.64). Without it you lose free will, because inhibition creates the delay between event and response in which you can think (p.64). It is built from six processes (pp.65–68).
2. **Named method/routine.** The six key processes of self-control (pp.65–68), in the book's order: (1) self-directed action; (2) aimed at changing subsequent behavior; (3) aimed at net gain of positive outcomes across short and long term; (4) depends on preferring larger delayed over smaller immediate rewards; (5) bridges the time lapse between event, response, outcome; (6) requires hindsight and foresight. Descriptive, not a routine to follow. Time: not stated.
3. **Tools/containers.** None named as products. Examples of self-directed actions: pausing, "writing yourself notes to contemplate and break down complex decisions and things to be done" (p.66), and making a "do list" of issues (p.66; Len's example: job, housing, transportation). Step Four "rules" promised to "trick your brain into exerting self-control" (p.65).
4. **Notes/thoughts/reference.** Only this: notes and lists are self-directed actions that break down complex decisions (p.66), and Len could "make some notes to pursue more information" before deciding (p.66). Where notes go/how stored: not addressed.
5. **Transfers/review.** Not addressed.
6. **Worksheets/deadlines.** None. Development claim: preference for larger delayed consequences rises from childhood to early 30s, paralleling frontal-lobe development; ADHD derails it (p.67).
7. **Key terms.**
- *Self-control*: "any response, or chain of responses, that we direct at ourselves that leads us to change our own behavior instead of just acting on impulse so that we can change what will happen in the future" (p.65).
- *Free will*: "just another way of saying we have the capacity to deliberate on our options to act" (p.65).
- *Cross-temporal organization of behavioral contingencies*: bridging the gap between event, response and outcome over long time spans; "job one of your executive brain (frontal lobes)"; "the ability to engage in time management or, more aptly, to manage yourself relative to the passage of time" (p.68).
- *Hindsight / foresight*: recall of the past gives hindsight; thinking about possible futures from it is foresight (p.68).
- *Carpe diem*: "great on vacation; it's terrible as a daily rule" (p.67).
