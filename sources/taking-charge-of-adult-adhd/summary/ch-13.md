# Chapter 13. The Stimulants
Pages 123–136.

1. **Main idea.** Stimulants (methylphenidate "MPH" and amphetamine "AMP") are the longest-used, most-known and most controversial ADHD medications: Schedule II, but "when they are taken orally as prescribed for ADHD the risk for addiction is low to nonsignificant" (p.123). They raise inhibition, resistance to distraction, sustained attention, motor timing and emotional self-control (p.123). This chapter summarizes the author's claims; it is not medical advice, and individual decisions belong with a prescriber.
2. **Named method/routine.** None. Delivery systems compared (pp.132–135): pills (absorbed in 15–20 min, peak 60–90 min, 3–5 hours; need 2–4 doses a day, p.132); the pump/Concerta (8–12 hours, p.133); pellets (8–10 hours; capsule can be opened onto applesauce/yogurt, pp.133–134); patch/Daytrana (remove several hours before bed; rash in 15–20%, p.134); pro-drug/Vyvanse (10–14 hours, p.135). Forgotten dose: if not past 1:00–2:00 P.M., take it; otherwise skip to next day (p.136). Tolerance: some feel reduced effect 3–6 months after starting; usual response is dose or form adjustment (p.136).
3. **Tools/containers.** None for organization. Brand/form table (p.124). Locked, safe storage so "no one but you can get" the medication (p.126). The book itself notes that remembering multiple daily doses is "probably not one of your strengths" (p.132) but offers no reminder tool.
4. **Notes/thoughts/reference.** Not addressed.
5. **Transfers/review.** Not addressed. Related: monitor for tics if there is a personal/family history (p.131); nonstimulants may be better if anxiety is a concern (p.129); ask to return to brand name if generic works worse (p.135).
6. **Worksheets/deadlines.** Common side effects listed most to least common (p.128): insomnia, loss of appetite, weight loss, headaches, nausea/stomachache, anxiety, irritability, motor tics, muscle tension. No worksheet.
7. **Key terms.**
- *Schedule II*: DEA class of potentially addictive medicines; strict limits on production, prescribing, storage (pp.123, 126).
- *Pro-drug*: active drug bonded to another compound so it cannot activate until split in the stomach/intestines (pp.134–135).
- *Mechanism analogy*: amphetamines "open a gate" (more dopamine released); methylphenidate "keeps the lock closed" (less reuptake) (pp.124–125).
- *Why not more antsy*: relevant brain regions are "underactive or underreactive" in ADHD (p.126).
Claims to treat as the author's: sudden death 1–3 per 100,000/year on stimulants vs 1–7 in general population (p.127); 16+ studies show no increased later drug abuse (p.127); up to a third with preexisting tics see them worsen (p.131). Pregnancy: all companies recommend stopping, which Barkley attributes partly to liability (p.128).
