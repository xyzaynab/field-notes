# Chapter 16. Rule 1: Stop the Action!
Pages 159–162. (Step Four, "Everyday Rules for Success"; opener pp.157–158.)

**Step Four opener (pp.157–158).** Medication may make you want to put the book down, but the eight rules "augment your treatment, kind of the way diet and exercise augment insulin" and do "cleanup" of residual symptoms (p.157). They are an "emergency tool kit"/"Plan B" for stress, illness, or reduced medication effect (pp.157–158). "Disorders belong to people. Handicaps belong to situations." (p.158) Use the rules when you need to get things done, avoid mistakes, be on time, learn from the past, reach goals (p.158).

1. **Main idea.** Rule 1 "buys you some time before you respond" (p.159): impulsivity is the root problem, so practise a small physical or verbal action that lets thinking start before you act (pp.159–162).
2. **Named method/routine.** Rule 1: Stop the Action. Steps in order: (a) identify where impulsivity hurts you most—work, dating, conversation, credit-card shopping, driving, other (pp.159–161); ask spouse/partner/friend/sibling to help and write the list (or have them write it) (p.160); (b) before entering such a situation, perform a simple action to stave off the urge to act—slow inhale/exhale, thoughtful face, say "Well, let me think about that"; or "Hmmmm, let me see now"; hand over mouth; hand on chin; key-turning motion behind back or in pocket (p.161); (c) paraphrase what the other person said ("You're asking me to…") to buy more seconds (pp.161–162); (d) say/do nothing else in those first seconds (p.162); (e) slow-talking model—"stop playing Tigger and become Winnie the Pooh"; practise speaking slowly in the mirror (p.162). Time: "a few seconds" per use (p.161); practice "every day, lots of times a day, even when you're alone" (p.162).
3. **Tools/containers, one job each.** A written list of costly situations (job: reminder "of where to be on guard and where to practice 'stopping the action' most", p.160); a helper (job: pinpoint trouble spots, p.160). No device named. Physical gestures act as cues, not tools.
4. **Notes/thoughts/reference.** The only written item is the list of costly impulsive situations, kept as a reminder (p.160). Other notes: not addressed.
5. **Transfers/review.** Not addressed; no stated review of the list.
6. **Worksheets/deadlines.** "Where is impulsivity a big problem for you?" prompts: work, dating, conversation, credit card, driving, other (pp.160–161). No deadline.
7. **Key terms.** *Stop the action*: do "any of the following simple actions" to buy seconds (p.161). Barkley: "The reason this little ploy works is that it does give you something to do while you're buying the time to think" (p.162). *Mental brake linings*: the capacity to stop (p.159).
