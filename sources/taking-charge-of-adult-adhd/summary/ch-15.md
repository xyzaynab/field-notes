# Chapter 15. What to Expect from Treatment
Pages 145–155. (Ends Step Three.)

1. **Main idea.** Finding the right medication is "as much art as science" (p.146) and takes patience: ~75% respond to the first drug tried; 80–90% respond to one of the available options (p.146); "up to 80%" chance of a life-changing course (p.145). Medication won't solve all problems, and therapists/coaches plus Steps Four–Five strategies fill the gaps (pp.151–152). Author's claims; medical decisions are for you and your doctor.
2. **Named method/routine.** Treatment sequence (pp.145–150): (1) physical exam and interview (heart rate, blood pressure, other medications; ECG optional); (2) rule out heart problems and drug interactions; (3) choose stimulant vs nonstimulant using the 16-item checklist (p.147); (4) titrate dose—start low, increase weekly until good response or limiting side effects (p.148); (5) switch delivery system/drug if needed; sequence suggested: methylphenidate form → other MPH form (Concerta/Daytrana) → amphetamine form (Adderall XR/Vyvanse) or atomoxetine → guanfacine XR → off-label bupropion (pp.148–149); (6) monitor. Time: dose finding "2–3 weeks or even 1–2 months" (p.148); weekly doctor contact early, then monthly for stimulants (prescription renewal, p.149), then follow-up every 3–6 months (p.150); extra adjustments often after the first 3–6 months (p.150). Doctors typically recommend medication 7 days a week, year-round (p.150). Treat the most impairing or life-threatening condition first (p.148).
3. **Tools/containers, one job each.**
- Stimulant-vs-nonstimulant checklist (p.147): help decide drug type; can be handed to the doctor (p.146). Rule: more "yes" answers → consider atomoxetine/nonstimulant first (p.148).
- **ADHD Symptom Tracking Scale** (pp.153–155): record symptoms and interference so you give the doctor specific information "far more … than you might recall without recording it" (p.150).
- A person who lives with or is close to you: give feedback on whether the drug is helping (p.148).
4. **Notes/thoughts/reference.** Only the scale as a recording tool (p.150). Where the filled scale goes: shared with your doctor (p.153). Otherwise not addressed.
5. **Transfers/review.** The completed scale is passed to the doctor at baseline, after starting, after a dose change, or after stopping (p.153). Follow-up exams every 3–6 months (p.150). No review step for personal notes.
6. **Worksheets/deadlines.** (a) Stimulant vs nonstimulant checklist, 16 yes/no items with totals (p.147). (b) ADHD Symptom Tracking Scale: Section I, 18 symptoms scored 0–3 (never/rarely to very often) with a check for time period (past 6 months baseline; since starting; since dose change; since stopping) (pp.153–154); Section II, 11 life areas scored 0–3 for interference (p.155). Guilford permits photocopying/downloading enlarged versions (p.154, box at end of Contents, unverified here). Tracking cadence: not stated.
7. **Key terms.**
- *Treatment is "as much art as science"* (p.146).
- *Delivery system, not the drug, might be the problem* (p.148).
- *Impairments vs ADHD-caused problems*: "In many cases, the first problem … may well be a consequence of having ADHD, while the second would not" (p.151).
- *Giving medication a helping hand*: therapists/coaches help, though "no scientific evidence this kind of treatment will suffice on its own" (p.152).
Anecdotes: a poet and a cellist chose to pause medication on creative days (pp.150–151); Barkley notes this is uncommon and undocumented in research (p.150).
