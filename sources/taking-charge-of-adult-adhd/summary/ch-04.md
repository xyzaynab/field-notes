# Chapter 4. What Do You Need for the Evaluation?
Pages 22–25.

1. **Main idea.** Knowing what the evaluation involves and bringing the right records and an open mind makes it smoother; no single test diagnoses ADHD, so the clinician weighs many sources (ch.4 pp.22–25).
2. **Named method/routine.** Evaluation elements (p.22): rating scales/referral info; interview; record review; psychological testing (cognitive delay/LDs); interviews with others who know you; general medical exam if medication is likely. Time: initial appointment "several hours" (p.23).
3. **Tools/containers.** None for personal organization. Things to bring (pp.22–23): school/medical/mental-health/driving/criminal records; names of a few trusted people; prior medical exam results; list of relatives with mental disorders; written description of childhood/adolescent and recent impairments.
4. **Notes/thoughts/reference.** Not addressed beyond gathering records and a written impairment description for the evaluator (pp.22–23).
5. **Transfers/review.** Not addressed.
6. **Worksheets/deadlines.** Bring-along list above; ask an older relative about family mental-health history before the appointment (p.24). Tests (p.25): brief IQ test; reading/math/spelling tests; attention/inhibition/memory tests—Barkley "do[es]n't recommend their use for diagnosing ADHD" (p.25); 35–85% of people with ADHD pass them (p.25).
7. **Key terms.** *Corroboration by others*: "there's strength in numbers" (p.23); the informant must be "in your corner" (p.24). *No diagnostic test*: "There is no diagnostic test for ADHD that is accurate enough to be used in clinical practice. Diagnosis is an art as well as a science." (p.25)
