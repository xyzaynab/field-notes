# Chapter 10. The Nature of ADHD and How You Can Master It
Pages 96–100. (First chapter with concrete tools.)

1. **Main idea.** ADHD is "a disorder of performance—of doing what you know rather than knowing what to do" (p.96). Treatment and tools work best when applied "at the point of performance in the natural environments" where problems occur; "the farther away in space and time a treatment is from this point, the less likely it is to help you" (p.97).
2. **Named method/routine.** A set of ten guidelines (pp.97–100), each a ✓ item, in the book's order: (1) become more aware of and inhibit your actions—transition planning (review rules before a situation, have the person repeat them back, review consequences) done by a partner; a nonverbal cue/word; daily check-ins with a coworker/supervisor on goals (pp.97–98); (2) externalize information usually held in mind (p.98); (3) make time physical (p.99); (4) use external incentives (p.99); (5) normalize the underlying deficits—medication (p.99); (6) replace distractions with physical cues (pp.99–100); (7) externalize your rules (p.100); (8) break tasks with large time gaps into smaller chunks (p.100); (9) stay flexible and change the plan (p.100). Counted in the text as a list of ✓ items; the book does not number or count them (unverified count: 9 listed). Time: check-ins "several times a day" (p.98); one step a day toward a month-long project (p.100); small reward every "hour or half hour of sustained work" (p.99).
3. **Tools/containers, one job each.**
- Small notebook + pen, or cell phone, carried in pocket: instantly capture a task, its steps, and deadline; kept "in front of you where your work is to be done" as "external working memory" (p.98).
- Day planner or to-do list: receive the task translated into smaller steps "as goals for each hour of the day and of the next few before the work is due" (p.98).
- Kitchen timer, computer appointment reminders, smartphone alarms, hour-by-hour calendar/day planner: make time physical (p.99).
- Small rewards (coffee, short tune, treat, checking a sports score): external incentive per small work quota (p.99). Caution: internet check "can lead to looking up 67 things" (p.99).
- Cards, lists, signs, sticky notes: physical cues replacing distractions at the task location (p.100).
- Posted signs/lists/charts, or an audio recorder with earphones: externalize rules (p.100).
- Medication: normalize executive-region function while in your system (p.99).
- A trusted other (partner, coworker, supervisor): accountability and cueing (p.98).
4. **Notes/thoughts/reference.** Tasks and instructions: write immediately in the pocket notebook/phone, then keep visible at the work location (p.98). Rules: make them physical lists posted in the relevant environment (p.100). Reference material: not addressed. The text says "The specific technique or external storage device here is not what is important—the principle behind it is!" (p.98).
5. **Transfers/review.** Task from notebook/phone → broken into smaller steps in day planner or to-do list with hour-by-hour goals (p.98). Review is implied by check-ins with a supervisor ("several times a day", p.98) and by "stay flexible… be prepared to change your plan" when breakthroughs occur (p.100); no scheduled review cadence for notes is given.
6. **Worksheets/deadlines.** None as worksheets. "Tools to get/use/Cues/Rewards" boxes only (pp.98–100). Deadline handling: write the deadline down at capture (p.98).
7. **Key terms.**
- *Point of performance*: where "you don't stop and think before you act and thus don't allow what you know to be activated" (p.97).
- *External working memory*: the written task/steps kept in view (p.98).
- *Offloading*: moving demands on working memory "onto some other storage device—in this case, paper or your cell phone" (p.98).
- *Motivational "prostheses"*: frequent external rewards, "nearly essential" for long projects (p.99).
- *Scaffolding*: aids to be kept "in place" (p.97).
- *Nearsighted to the future / blind to time* (p.96).
Also: Barkley says "avoid talk- or insight-oriented therapy, psychoanalysis, weekly group therapy focusing on complaining" because there's no evidence for intervention outside points of performance (p.100).
