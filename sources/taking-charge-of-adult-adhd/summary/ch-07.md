# Chapter 7. Resisting Impulses: The First Step in Self-Control
Pages 58–63.

1. **Main idea.** Many problems labeled "difficulty paying attention" are actually impulse-control problems: the person cannot resist attending to whatever is more interesting (pp.59–60). Poor inhibition shows as distractibility (Dan), acting too fast (Shayla), and inability to stop (Jess) (pp.58–63). "The 'time blindness' of ADHD starts with a lack of impulse control" (p.63).
2. **Named method/routine.** None. Pointers: improving impulse control "starts with changing your brain: See Step Three"; "Compensating is all about buying yourself time: See Step Four" (p.63).
3. **Tools/containers.** None.
4. **Notes/thoughts/reference.** Not addressed.
5. **Transfers/review.** Not addressed.
6. **Worksheets/deadlines.** Reflection prompts only ("Do you identify with Dan's list? What would you add?", pp.59, 62, 63). No deadline.
7. **Key terms.**
- *Inhibition*: "the foundation of tact. It helps create a pause in our automatic actions" (p.58).
- *Perseveration*: what "some advocates … call 'hyperfocusing'"; "researchers call this problem perseveration"; it "often looks like procrastination" but is deciding "to keep going on something that is fun" (p.63).
- *Brain timing explanation*: primary motor system matures too early; the higher-level controlling center matures too late (p.60; cites NIMH study on p.111, unverified here).
