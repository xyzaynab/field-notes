# Chapter 9. Executive Functions: The Seven Abilities That Make Up Self-Control … and More
Pages 69–95.

1. **Main idea.** Self-control is carried out by seven executive functions—self-directed actions that are "obvious, public behavior early in child development" and become internalized ("thinking") in adulthood (p.70). ADHD delays each one, so adults with ADHD act out thoughts instead of keeping them in mind (p.70). Knowing which function is weakest tells you "which type of self-control to target" (p.70) and helps you "choose external tools and strategies" (p.72).
2. **Named method/routine.** The seven executive functions, in the book's order (p.70; summary list p.95): self-awareness ("the mind's mirror"), inhibition ("brakes"), nonverbal working memory ("eye"), verbal working memory ("voice"), emotion regulation ("heart"), self-motivation ("fuel tank"), planning/problem solving ("playground"). They develop one at a time and shift control from external to internal, others to self, present to future, instant to deferred gratification (pp.72–73). One embedded procedure: the six-step problem-solving "meta-rule": state the problem; list solutions; critique each; select one; implement; evaluate (p.83). Time: not stated.
3. **Tools/containers recommended.** Mostly mental "tools": mind's voice/self-talk (job: formulate rules and plans, calm emotions, pp.83, 88); visual imagery (job: map to the future, imitation, calming via positive memories, pp.76–78, 90); a written **to-do list** (job: make self-statements/rules external "to help us remember and use later", p.83); automatic payroll deposit (job: remove the need for restraint, p.85); invisible-key gesture as an example trick (p.71). Specific physical organizing tools: not addressed here (Step Four).
4. **Notes/thoughts/reference.** Only: rules and self-statements are written on a to-do list (p.83). Examples of failure: Marta rebuilds family filing system every couple of years and gets lost in paper (p.94). No storage system is prescribed. Otherwise not addressed.
5. **Transfers/review.** One example: Carmelita could "review her spending/saving plan every month" (p.85). Nina's meta-rule: see a physician before dieting and remove fattening foods from the home (p.85). No general review step.
6. **Worksheets/deadlines.** Reflective checklists under each "How ADHD interferes with…" heading to find your weakest function (pp.79–81 nonverbal WM; 84–87 verbal WM; 91–92 emotion/motivation; 94 planning). Time-horizon claims: 3-year-old vs 14-year-old (a day or two) vs 36-year-old (6–12 weeks) (p.72); adults 20–30 organize behavior 8–12 weeks ahead (p.78). No deadline.
7. **Key terms.**
- *Executive functions*: "the actions directed at ourselves, the mental activities we engage in when we think about our future" (p.69).
- *Internalizing/privatizing*: actions become unobservable "thinking", because the signal is inhibited from reaching behavior (p.70).
- *Self-awareness*: first executive function to emerge; "the mind's mirror" (pp.73–74). Deficit: unaware of "how you're acting… and what the predictable consequences would be" (p.75).
- *Nonverbal working memory*: "capacity to hold information in mind—not through words but through your senses" (p.76); gives imagery, imitation, hindsight/foresight, sense of time (pp.76–78).
- *Hindsight → Foresight → Preparation to act* (p.78).
- *Time management*: the capacity "to sense and judge the flow of time and manage ourselves relative to it"; "one of the strongest predictors of educational and occupational success" (p.78).
- *Vicarious learning*: copying the good and avoiding what failed in others (p.77).
- *Verbal working memory*: "the mind's voice"; talking to yourself, silent by ages 7–9 (p.82).
- *Meta-rules*: "rules about rules" (p.83).
- *Emotion regulation*: lets us "soothe ourselves", "distract ourselves", "implement an alternative emotion", "choose a more moderate emotional tone" (p.90).
- *Self-motivation*: "the mind's fuel tank"; combined with emotional self-control in the author's account (p.88).
- *Planning/problem solving*: "mental play"; "Play is training for inventiveness in adult problem solving" (p.93).
Other: until age 30 or older adults with ADHD "cannot be trusted to be the sole judge" of their own symptoms; DSM-5 now requires corroboration (pp.75–76).
